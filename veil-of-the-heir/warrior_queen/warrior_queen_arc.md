"Thou hast changed," he said. "Not merely the body — though the body hath changed enough that the soldiers barely recognise the prince who stood in this yard a month ago. Thy stance is different. Thy balance. The way thou dost enter a room, draw a blade, give an order. Thou art becoming something I have not seen in many years."

"What is that?"

He poked the fire with a stick. Sparks rose like tiny stars.

"When I was young," he said, "I served thy grandmother. Queen Velathra. The Last Steel Queen, they called her, because she was the last to ride to war in full armour. She fought at the Battle of the Crimson Pass with a longsword in one hand and a torch in the other, and the enemy broke before her like waves upon a cliff." He paused. "She was not a gentle woman. She was not a kind woman. But she was a warrior, and the kingdom knew that whatever darkness came, their queen would stand between them and the abyss with steel in her hand."

He looked at me.

"After Velathra, the warrior queens faded. Thy mother is formidable — but she leadeth from the throne, not the saddle. The sword hath been replaced by the sceptre. The armour by the gown. The warrior queen became a memory."

He poked the fire again. More sparks.

"Until now."

I sat with the weight of that statement for a long time. The fire warmed my face, which was softer than it had been thirty days ago — the jawline more delicate, the cheekbones higher, the lips fuller. The body beneath the armour was measurably different — the breasts filling the adapted breastplate, the waist narrower, the hips broader, the frame lighter and more flexible. The hair fell past my shoulder blades. The voice, when I spoke, carried notes that had not been there a month ago — higher, clearer, the baritone nearly gone.

Thirty days. Halfway through the arc. And the person sitting beside this fire was not the person who had stood in this yard on Day 1, humiliated and furious and terrified.

"I am afraid," I said. The words came out before I could catch them — slipping past the guard I maintained before Aldric, before the court, before the world.

He did not dismiss the fear. He did not mock it. He did not tell me to be strong.

"Good," he said. "The day thou art not afraid is the day thou becomest dangerous — not to thine enemies, but to thyself. Fear is the whetstone upon which courage is sharpened. Without it, bravery is merely recklessness, and recklessness killeth queens."

He stood. Brushed the soot from his leathers. Looked down at me in the firelight — the scarred, battle-hardened, emotionally arid wall of a man who had, over thirty days, become something I had not expected.

A mentor. A guardian. A friend.

"Thirty more days," he said. "And when they are done, thou shalt not merely be a warrior who is becoming a queen. Thou shalt be a queen who was always a warrior."

He walked away into the dark. I sat beside the fire and let the tears come — not the hot, angry tears of the first days, not the frustrated tears of a body that would not obey, but the quiet, cleansing tears of someone who was beginning to understand what she was becoming and was, for the first time, grateful for it.

The fire burned low. The stars wheeled overhead. And somewhere in the castle, Aldric sharpened Frostfang and said nothing to anyone about what he had seen in the heir's eyes.

Because some things are too valuable for words.

### ⚔️ DAY 31 — The New Armour

**Location:** Forge → Iron Garden

Brannic and Eolla Copperwright unveiled their masterwork.

They had been working since Day 5, when Veletia had delivered the measurements and Aldric had delivered the specifications. Now, thirty-one days into the arc, they presented the result: a full suit of armour designed from the first rivet for the body I was becoming.

It was breathtaking. Eolla's artistry — the filigree along the pauldrons, the silver inlay at the collar, the Velmoria crest worked into the breastplate in midnight blue enamel — married to Brannic's engineering. The steel was lighter than standard plate, hardened through a process Brannic refused to explain beyond grunting that it involved dragonmoss resin and a week of cursing. The breastplate was shaped — not the cartoonish breast-shaped cups of fantasy, but a subtle, practical curve that provided clearance for the chest without creating weak points in the steel. The waist articulated. The hip guards accommodated the wider pelvis. The greaves fit the changed calves. Every piece accounted for the body that was, not the body that had been.

I armoured up with Veletia's help. The training corset fit beneath the breastplate seamlessly — Eolla had designed the interior dimensions to accommodate it. The weight settled onto my shoulders and hips with a balance that my old armour had never achieved, because the old armour had been designed for a body I no longer possessed and this armour was designed for the body I did.

I moved. I stretched. I drew the rapier and assumed the feminine guard.

The armour moved with me.

Not against me, as every piece of equipment had since the transformation began — the old leathers that bound the chest, the chainmail that hung wrong on narrowed shoulders, the boots that were loose on shrinking feet. This armour followed the body's lines, supported its curves, protected its vulnerabilities. When I cross-stepped, the hip guards pivoted. When I drop-stepped, the knee plates flexed. When I lunged, the breastplate absorbed the breast's movement without compression or bounce.

"How doth it feel?" Brannic asked, his soot-covered face unusually anxious.

"Like it was made for me," I said.

"It was," Eolla said quietly, her gem-studded fingers laced together. "Every piece. Every rivet. For thee."

I wore the new armour for the entire training session. Aldric tested it — strikes to every plate, every joint, every potential weak point. The steel held. The articulation held. The fit held.

At the end of the session, I saluted Brannic and Eolla with the rapier — a formal gesture, one warrior to the artisans who armed her.

Brannic's eyes went suspiciously bright. He muttered something about forge dust and turned away.

Eolla simply smiled and said, "Wear it well, Your Highness. It is the finest work we have ever done."

### ⚔️ DAY 32 — The Farmstead Defense

**Location:** Widow Maren's farmstead, two hours west

A farmstead on the western road had been raided overnight. Livestock slaughtered, grain stores burned, the farmer's widow — a woman named Maren, sixty years old and made of the same unyielding material as the mountain stone — beaten and bound in her own home.

Bandits. Not the organized rogues of Millhaven Road or the desperate girl of Fernwick, but a crueler breed — deserters from a disbanded mercenary company, men with training and weapons and the particular savagery of soldiers who had been released from discipline and kept only the violence.

Six of them. They had made camp in the farmstead's barn, drinking Maren's cider and laughing about the old woman they had left tied to her bedpost.

I arrived with Torin and six soldiers. We surrounded the barn at dusk.

I stood before the barn door in the new armour — the silver filigree catching the fading light, the Velmoria crest blazing on the breastplate, the rapier drawn and the dagger ready. Behind me, soldiers blocked every exit. Torin lurked in the shadows with the patience of a man who had elevated lurking to an art form.

"Come out," I called. "I am Caelan of Velmoria, Heir to the Throne. Lay down thy arms and submit to the crown's justice."

Laughter from within. A voice — deep, rough, slurred with drink — called back: "The prince with the tits! Come in and show us what thou'rt growing, pretty boy!"

I kicked the barn door open.

The six mercenaries were scattered within — most seated, most drunk, all armed. The one who had spoken was the largest, a brute with a shaved head and a scar across his nose, holding a war hammer in one hand and a jug of cider in the other.

He looked at me. At the armour shaped for a woman's body. At the rapier in my hand. At the long hair and the changing face and the budding chest beneath the fitted breastplate.

He laughed again. "Gods preserve us. 'Tis true. The prince hath gone and grown himself a pair of — "

The rapier took the jug from his hand. Not his hand — the jug. A flick of the wrist, a slash through the handle, and the cider vessel fell in two pieces. His hand remained intact, though the knuckle that had held the handle bore a thin red line where the blade had kissed the skin.

The laughter stopped.

"I will not ask again," I said.

Four of the mercenaries laid down their weapons. The fifth and sixth — including the brute — charged.

The barn fight was savage, close, and exactly the kind of brawling, ugly combat that the feminine style was not designed for. But I had run the gauntlet. I had fought goblins in dens and Murkkin on bridges and Griln in dark forests. I had been struck, bruised, bled, and I had learned that the body's discomfort was temporary and the crown's justice was not.

The brute swung the war hammer. I drop-stepped — the blessed drop-step, my savior, my signature — and the hammerhead whistled over me. I rose into the thrust and put the rapier through his thigh, severing the muscle above the knee. He fell screaming. The sixth mercenary came at me from the side, and Torin materialized to meet him with a blade across the forearm that ended the matter instantly.

We bound the mercenaries. Freed Maren. The old widow looked at me from her doorstep, rubbing her raw wrists, her face bruised but her spine unbowed.

"I know thy mother," she said. "Hard woman. Good queen. I see her in thee."

"I hope so," I said.

"Do not hope," Maren said. "Be."

### ⚔️ DAY 33 — Swimming and the Changing Body

**Location:** River Vel, south bend

Aldric declared that a warrior queen must be able to fight in water — not because aquatic combat was common, but because rivers crossed battlefields, rain flooded camps, and a queen who could not swim in armour was a queen who drowned.

The training took place in the River Vel's south bend, where the current was gentle and the water waist-deep. I stripped to the training corset and trousers — the armour would come later — and waded in.

The cold water hit my body with an intimacy that was shocking in its thoroughness. Every change the transformation had wrought was suddenly, vividly present — the water finding the new curves, flowing over the breasts and around the hips, pressing against the softer stomach, channeling differently along thighs that were thicker and legs that were shaped for a different gait. The body's buoyancy had changed. The fat distribution — breasts, hips, thighs — altered the way I floated, tipping me onto my back more readily, making the old chest-first swimming stroke less efficient.

I had to relearn how to swim. Not entirely — the mechanics were similar — but the body moved through water differently now. The narrower shoulders reduced my stroke width. The wider hips created more drag. The breasts, even compressed in the wet corset, added a chest profile that the water resisted.

Aldric stood on the riverbank, dry, arms folded, watching with the expression of a man who was aware he was too old and too dignified to wade into a river for a swimming lesson.

"Stop fighting the water," he called. "The river is not thy enemy. Work with the current, not against it."

I adjusted. Instead of the powerful, shoulders-forward crawl I had used as a prince, I shifted to a hip-driven side-stroke that used the lower body's new power. The wider hips, which hindered the crawl, became propulsive in the side-stroke — the scissor kick was stronger, driven by thighs that were thicker and a pelvis that generated more rotational force.

By midday I was swimming smoothly in the current, the corset waterlogged and heavy but manageable, the rapier secured at my hip in a waterproof sheath. I practiced drawing the blade underwater — a clumsy, slow-motion affair that would be useless in any real combat but taught me the feel of the weapon in a wet hand.

The most absurdly funny moment came when I attempted to climb out of the river onto a steep bank. The wet corset had loosened, the trousers were heavy with water, and my hair — soaking, three times its dry weight, plastered across my face and shoulders — conspired with the slippery mud to turn my exit into a Sisyphean comedy. I hauled myself halfway up, slid back. Hauled again, slid again. On the third attempt, I reached the top, planted one hand on the grass, and the riverbank crumbled.

I fell face-first back into the River Vel with a splash that sent a wave halfway across the channel.

I surfaced, spitting water and mud, to find Torin extending a hand from the bank, his face — normally an impenetrable mask of stoic devotion — visibly struggling against what could only be described as the most powerful urge to laugh he had ever experienced.

"Not a word," I said.

Torin pulled me out. His hand engulfed mine — my hand, smaller than it had been, the fingers thinner, the grip requiring both his strength and my desperation. We stood on the bank, both dripping, and for a moment our eyes met and something passed between us — warmth, amusement, and a tenderness that Torin buried immediately beneath his granite exterior.

Aldric, from his dry vantage, said: "Tomorrow we add armour."

I wrung out my hair. It took both hands.

### ⚔️ DAY 34 — The Shadebound Changeling

**Location:** North Forest — deep interior

Intelligence reported a Shadebound Changeling — the most dangerous creature in the North Forest's bestiary — had been sighted near the village of Willowmark, three leagues north. A shadow-born shapeshifter that could mimic faces, voices, and forms. It fed on doubt, identity confusion, and the psychic energy of people who were uncertain of themselves.

Which meant, as Aldric pointed out with his customary tact, that a person in the midst of a magical gender transformation was essentially a walking buffet for the creature.

"It will target thee," he said. "It will take thy face — thy old face, the prince's face — and use it against thee. It will speak with thy old voice. It will show thee what thou wert and ask if what thou art becoming is worth the cost."

I gripped the rapier. "Let it try."

We entered the deep forest with a squad of eight. The Changeling's presence was felt before it was seen — a creeping wrongness in the air, a sensation that the shadows were too deep, the sounds too muted, the forest too still. The hairs on my arms rose. The skin prickled. Even the breasts, compressed in the corset beneath the new armour, seemed to tighten, the tissue contracting as though trying to withdraw into the body.

The Changeling struck at noon, in a clearing that should have been bright with sunlight but was instead drowning in shadow.

It stepped from the darkness wearing my face.

Not my current face — my old face. The prince's face. Square-jawed, broad-browed, short-haired, hard-eyed. The body it wore was my old body — broad shoulders, flat chest, narrow hips, the masculine frame I had trained in and fought in and lived in for twenty years.

It smiled with my old smile. It spoke with my old voice — the deep, certain baritone that I had lost to the transformation, the voice I sometimes still expected to hear when I opened my mouth.

"Look at thee," it said, and the prince's voice was warm and pitying and terrible. "Look at what they have done to thee. The tits. The hips. The soft hands, the thin arms, the hair like a maiden's. They have taken everything thou wert and made thee into a doll for a crown."

I raised the rapier. My hand trembled.

The Changeling laughed — my old laugh, rich and masculine and confident — and spread the prince's arms wide. "Come now. Thou dost not truly wish to fight me. I am thee. The real thee. The thee before the potions and the dresses and the giggling. Before they reshaped thy body into something thou canst barely recognise. Dost thou not miss me?"

The soldiers behind me were frozen. Some could see the resemblance. Others saw only a shadow-born monster wearing a stolen face. Torin's hand was on his sword, but his eyes — his grey, burning, devoted eyes — were on me, waiting.

I looked at the Changeling. At the prince's face. At the broad shoulders and the flat chest and the square jaw and the confidence — that absolute, unshakeable confidence — of a body that had never once been forced to question what it was.

And I said: "I do not miss thee. I have surpassed thee."

The rapier struck.

The Changeling was fast — shadow-fast, the blade passing through the space where the prince's image had been and meeting only air. It reformed behind me, wearing my mother's face now, the Queen's imperious gaze boring into me.

"Foolish child," it said, in my mother's voice. "Thou wilt never be enough. Not man enough, not woman enough, not queen enough — "

I pivoted. The feminine pivot-retreat, spinning on the hip, the rapier extending. The blade caught the Changeling's shoulder — a solid hit that trailed shadow-stuff rather than blood, dark wisps that dissolved in the air.

The creature screamed — a sound that was no longer any stolen voice but its own, a thin, reedy shriek of pain and fury. It shifted again, cycling through faces — Aldric's, Maera's, Therion's, Serelyne's — each one speaking a different fear, a different doubt.

I stopped listening. I breathed into the belly, as Aldric had taught me on Day 2. I found the center, the root, the place where the feminine guard drew its strength. And I fought the Changeling not as the prince it tried to remind me I had been, and not as the queen it tried to convince me I could never be, but as the warrior I was in this moment — imperfect, unfinished, changing, afraid, and absolutely, ferociously alive.

The killing blow came on the drop-step. The Changeling, wearing its own face at last — a blank, featureless oval of living shadow — lunged for my throat. I dropped. The shadow-hands passed through the air above my head. I rose, the rapier driving upward into the creature's core, and Thalara's voice echoed in my memory: *The heart of a Changeling is its doubt. Pierce the doubt, and the shadow dies.*

The rapier found something solid inside the shadow — not flesh, not bone, but a knot of condensed darkness, a pearl of crystallized fear. The blade pierced it.

The Changeling dissolved. The shadows lifted. The clearing filled with sunlight.

I stood in the brightness, the rapier dripping shadow-ichor, my hair streaming in a wind that had not been there moments before. The soldiers stared. Torin sheathed his sword, his face pale with an emotion he would never name.

Aldric stepped forward. He looked at the dissolving remains of the creature. He looked at me.

"It showed thee the prince," he said. Not a question.

"Aye."

"And?"

I cleaned the rapier on a leaf. The shadow-stuff evaporated in the sunlight, leaving the steel bright and clean.

"And the prince was not enough," I said. "He was never enough. He just did not know it yet."

Aldric held my gaze for a long, heavy moment. Then he turned to the soldiers and began issuing orders for the return march, as though the heir to the throne had not just killed a psychic horror by stabbing her own past through the heart.

Some things do not require commentary. They simply require acknowledgment.

And Aldric acknowledged by doing what he always did: moving forward.

### ⚔️ DAY 35 — Dame Yseldra's Challenge

**Location:** Castle Archery Range → Eastern Battlements

Whether that was a compliment to my progress or a calculated political move to cement the warrior-queen image, I neither knew nor cared. The bow was in my hands, the targets were set, and I intended to shoot well enough that the question was irrelevant.

The higher elbow was habit. The breast guard — a fitted piece of boiled leather beneath the corset — protected the tissue without restricting the draw.

I shot fifty arrows. The wind was cruel — gusting from the east, swirling over the battlements, catching the shafts and bending them from true. I read the flags. I timed the releases. I breathed into the belly and let the arrow fly at the moment the wind held its breath.

Yseldra shot fifty arrows with the warbow. Her draw was magnificent — ninety pounds pulled to the cheek with an ease that suggested the bow was merely an inconvenience between her hand and the target. Her arrows flew harder, faster, flatter.

Thirty-six inner rings. Eleven bullseyes.

The range fell silent. Yseldra lowered her bow. She looked at the target. She looked at mine. She looked at me.

"Well," she said. "Fuck."

The range exploded. Soldiers cheered. Nobles applauded. Jeral, who had been keeping score on a scroll with the meticulous precision of a man who approached everything as though it were a matter of state, pushed his spectacles up his nose and announced the results with barely concealed glee.

Yseldra crossed the range to where I stood. "Thy mother's eye," she said again. "Better than thy mother's eye."

"Granted," I said.

She nodded — curt, military, but with a warmth behind it that had not been there thirty-five days ago — and departed.

I stood at the range, the composite bow in my hands, and let the applause wash over me like warm rain. The body that held the bow was different from the body that had held the first bow on Day 4 — rounder, softer in places, stronger in others, the breasts now filling the breast guard with enough presence to require genuine accommodation, the hips wider, the stance lower, the entire frame redesigned by alchemy and training into something that was neither prince nor princess but something rarer.

A warrior queen. Becoming.
