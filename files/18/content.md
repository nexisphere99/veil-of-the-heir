At the bottom of the curtsey, my back knee hovered an inch above the ground. My thighs burned. My core engaged to maintain the vertical spine. The corset held me rigid through what was, essentially, a very deep squat in formal clothing.

The court skirt's train added a dimension of complexity to the curtsey that bordered on the architectural. Ascending stairs in the ankle-length skirt required a technique I had not anticipated: the lift-and-step.

In trousers, climbing stairs was automatic — foot up, plant, push, next foot. The fabric moved with the legs without interference. In the ankle-length skirt, the hem caught at the ankle on the ascending foot's upward motion, pulling the fabric taut across the knee and threatening to pitch me face-first into the steps.

<<speech "Veletia" "The left hand gathereth the front panel of the skirt — just the front, not the sides — and lifteth it to ankle height. No higher. Lifting the skirt above the ankle is permissible only when crossing puddles or mud, and even then, thou shalt receive disapproving looks from Lady Serelyne. The right hand resteth upon the banister, or, if no banister existeth, hangeth at the side. The body ascendeth with the spine straight — the corset will ensure this — and each step is planted flat, not toe-first. Flat planting preventeth the heel from catching the hem behind thee.">>

I gathered the front panel. The fabric bunched in my left hand — smooth, warm, the wool familiar from hours of handling. I lifted it to ankle height, revealing the slippers beneath, and climbed.

The first step was cautious. The second was steadier. The third deposited me on the platform without incident, the skirt released from my hand, the fabric falling back to its full length as I straightened.

<<speech "Veletia" "Good. Now descend.">>

Descending was harder. The instinct was to look down — to watch the feet, to see where the steps were, to ensure the hem was not underfoot. But Veletia's instruction was absolute: a queen does not look down. She looks forward. She trusts her feet to find the steps. She trusts the skirt to follow. She trusts the architecture of her body and her clothing to carry her safely from height to floor.

I descended. The left hand gathered the front panel again. The right hand found the banister — a concession to practicality that Veletia permitted for training purposes but expected me to abandon within a week. Each step was flat-planted, as instructed, the heel touching first, the toe following, the weight shifting forward and down with the controlled precision of a combat descent.

The ankle-length skirt's hem grazed the steps' edges as I descended, the fabric brushing the wood with a whisper that sounded, in the gallery's quiet, like the rustle of pages turning. The sound was surprisingly pleasant — a textile murmur that accompanied each step like a quiet heartbeat.

I reached the bottom. Turned — the smooth, sweeping turn I had practiced earlier. And faced the mirror at the gallery's end.

In the mirror, a woman in grey wool descended invisible stairs, her spine straight, her hand holding the fabric with casual grace, her face forward, her eyes ahead. She did not look down. She trusted.

<<speech "Veletia" "Now. The court skirt. With train.">>

The court skirt and its three-foot train turned the stair challenge into a military operation. Ascending required the left hand to gather the front panel while the right hand reached behind to lift the train clear of the steps — two separate motions, performed simultaneously, while climbing, while keeping the spine straight, while not looking down.

Descending was worse. The train, trailing behind, had to be managed so that it draped down the steps behind the body rather than pooling on the step currently occupied — pooling meant stepping on the train, which meant falling, which meant dying of embarrassment before the entire court, which Veletia informed me had actually happened to a duchess in the southern kingdoms two centuries ago, although she had the good grace to merely break her wrist rather than perish.

I practiced the court-skirted stair ascent and descent twenty-one times. I caught the train twice. I stumbled once. By the fifteenth repetition, the dual-handed management of front panel and train was becoming automatic — the same kind of muscle memory that the dual-blade technique had required, the left hand and the right hand performing different tasks simultaneously, independently, as though belonging to two different people sharing the same body.

By the twenty-first, I descended the three wooden steps in the full court skirt with a smoothness that surprised even Veletia, who stopped mid-sip of her afternoon tea and watched me arrive at the bottom with the expression of a woman who had just seen her finest dress walk itself off the mannequin and come to life.
